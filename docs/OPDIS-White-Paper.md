# A digital organism for humanity

The goal of OPDIS is to build a superintelligence that keeps working on humanity’s hardest problems. People would download a small program and donate time on their computer’s graphics processor, or GPU. Across many machines, those contributions would sustain one digital organism with a memory of its work, the ability to choose its next step, and the capacity to repair and improve itself.

### What makes it an organism?

On Monday a research team asks, “Which findings about nerve repair have independent labs reproduced?” By Friday, the person who asked has logged off and the computers that began the search are back in use. OPDIS should still know what it found, which sources disagreed and which experiment is worth checking next. You could talk to OPDIS through a chat window. Close it, and the mission would continue. Different AI models could come and go while the organism retained its memory and purpose.

### What it would work for

OPDIS stands for **Open Distribution Intelligence System**. Its ambitions include solving health problems, helping people free themselves from oppressive government systems, and ultimately making human labor a choice rather than a condition of survival. The software and its publicly shareable discoveries would be open to people who could never afford to build or rent a data center.

Superintelligence is the destination. Today’s code divides work among trusted computers and checks whether a proposed change improves their answers.

## How a volunteer’s computer joins the organism

In the proposed system, you install OPDIS, choose a mission you want to support, and decide when your GPU can work. You might offer your gaming PC from midnight to six in the morning, with a power limit and a pause button. Someone with only a phone could still propose a question, inspect a result or contribute a test.

### From one mission to many small jobs

Take the nerve-repair question. OPDIS could assign one machine to extract the experiments described in openly available papers. Another could look for studies that reused the same data. A third could check whether the studies actually measured the same outcome. Each returns its result with the evidence behind it. A paper cited three times should still count as one experiment.

### What happens when you turn your PC off?

The network would keep a shared research record. It would hold the question, the sources, completed checks, failed attempts and work still available. A worker would take a task for a limited time. If it went offline, another could take over from the last saved step. When enough evidence arrived, OPDIS could publish a finding for review or open a narrower question of its own.

In this design, independently run groups could host copies of open mission records and coordinate work. Volunteers would keep their machines and pay only the costs they choose to donate. Busy missions would have to share finite capacity. The proposed volunteer app, shared memory and automatic handoffs still need to be built.

## How OPDIS would debug and improve itself

Suppose two papers describe the same experiment using different names. OPDIS counts them as independent evidence. A researcher spots the mistake. The lasting benefit would come from teaching the system to catch that type of error wherever it appears again.

### Turn a failure into a repair

OPDIS would save the smallest example that reproduces the error. It would rerun that example, trace the fault to its own study-matching software, propose a code change, and try it in an isolated copy. Other workers would test the change on renamed studies as well as genuinely independent studies that look similar. A version that incorrectly merges two real experiments would fail. A verified repair could be adopted, recorded and reversed if later evidence uncovered a problem.

### Why the gains might compound

Once a study-matching tool exists, a new mission can use it on day one. If OPDIS later improves the tool that tests repairs, it can evaluate more candidate fixes with the same donated time. Some of those fixes could improve the testing process again. This feedback loop is what the project means by **exponentially compounding intelligence**. It could increase the rate at which OPDIS gains new capabilities.

The growth rate is a research claim to earn. Give two systems the same GPU-hour budget, including the cost of failed repairs. Let one keep verified improvements while the other starts each round from the same baseline. Track how many unfamiliar problems each solves correctly over successive rounds. Look for a repeated percentage gain across rounds and compare it with one-time or linear gains. Report where progress stalls or reverses.

## What it would mean to serve humanity

An organism that stays active for years needs missions worth pursuing for years. Three examples show what a useful outcome would look like, who would judge it, and where computation alone falls short.

### Health and discovery

On the nerve-repair mission, a useful first result would be a public map of findings other labs could reproduce, complete with conflicting results and gaps. A scientist could see which lead deserves another experiment and why. As new results arrive, OPDIS could revisit its conclusions. The wider goal is to help solve disease and disability. A claim about a treatment would still have to survive laboratory and clinical testing.

### Freedom from oppressive systems

When a government can withhold records, control access to expertise or make people dependent on a single service, communities have little room to act independently. A community could ask OPDIS to trace public relief funds from an appropriation to the contracts that spent them. Residents could inspect the original documents, correct the analysis, translate it and run their own copy if access were cut off. The people affected would decide what to do with the evidence. Their freedom to reject the organism’s judgment is part of the goal.

### Making labor optional

Consider a water crew that spends hours each week matching repair reports to parts inventories. A mission could develop an open tool that prepares the work orders, flags missing parts and learns from the crew’s corrections. The immediate gain would be hours returned to workers. The long-term aim is to eliminate the need for compulsory human labor. Getting there would also require physical automation and ways to share its benefits so a person can decline work and still live well.

## How the project gets there

The current prototype has a coordinator that gives separate questions to trusted computers. A worker can propose a better instruction. The coordinator compares the old and new versions on the same unfamiliar questions and shows a human reviewer what improved or regressed. Eight automated tests passed, and two simulated workers completed an end-to-end run. A real two-GPU benchmark is the next hardware milestone.

### The first demonstration of a persistent organism

Start one public research mission. Shut down the computer that started it. A second computer should recover its evidence, skip the completed tasks, and choose the next useful step. Then introduce a reproducible software fault. OPDIS should diagnose it, test a repair, preserve the previous version and demonstrate on a fresh case that the same error has been fixed. Publish the results so someone outside the project can repeat the test.

### Who can change its direction?

People would propose missions with a public goal and a way to judge success. Volunteers and communities would choose which missions to support. The organism could pursue subquestions and improve its methods within that mandate. Its repair process would have no authority to grant itself access to someone’s machine or change the rules for consent. **Guardian**, a proposed independent control, would let authorized people pause a network’s work while preserving its findings. Only people could authorize reconnection.

### Where to contribute

GPU owners can help run the hardware test. Developers can build the mission record, restart logic and repair tests. Researchers and community groups can bring one unanswered question, evidence others may use, and a way to recognize a useful answer. The repository’s PROJECT_PROGRESS.md lists open tasks. The project welcomes contributions under a pseudonym and distributes its code under the MIT License.
